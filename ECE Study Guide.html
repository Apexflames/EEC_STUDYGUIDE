Science and Nature in ECE

Importance of Science and Nature in ECE

Why science matters, nature education, theories, the teacher's role, integration, and the trial questions.

Importance of science and nature in ECE

Science is an integral part of young children's daily lives. From watching a butterfly splash in a puddle to seeing a seed sprout into a plant, children are engaging in natural scientific enquiry. ECE provides the ideal environment to nurture and channel this innate curiosity into structured, meaningful learning.

Plain English
Science in ECE is not about memorising facts. It is about building a scientific habit of mind: observation, questioning, experimenting and drawing conclusions.

6Connection to everyday lifeExplains why it rains, how plants grow, what floats or sinks, so learning is meaningful and relevant.

Nature education in ECE

Nature education (nature-based learning or outdoor learning) is the intentional use of natural environments and natural materials to support children's development. It includes gardening, animal care, nature walks, weather observations and environmental stewardship.

The role of the ECE teacher

The teacher is a facilitator and co-inquirer, not a dispenser of knowledge.

  1. Set up inviting, stimulating science environments, indoors and outdoors.
  2. Ask open-ended questions: 'What do you notice?', 'What do you think will happen if...?'
  3. Listen carefully to children's ideas and use them as starting points.
  4. Document learning through photographs, drawings, anecdotal records and learning stories.
  5. Model scientific attitudes: curiosity, patience, honesty, openness to unexpected results.
  6. Connect science to other curriculum areas such as literacy, mathematics and the arts.

Goals and History

Rationale for Teaching Science in ECE

Goals, objectives, history and the theories behind science in ECE.

Goals and objectives of ECE science education

Broad goal: to foster scientific literacy in young children.

  1. Develop positive attitudes towards science and enquiry.
  2. Nurture curiosity, creativity and a love of learning.
  3. Promote awareness of the environment and sustainability.

Specific objectives:

  1. Develop basic science process skills (observing, classifying, measuring, predicting).
  2. Introduce simple scientific concepts appropriate to developmental stages.
  3. Encourage hands-on exploration and manipulation of materials.
  4. Support development of scientific language and communication.
  5. Promote cooperative learning through group science activities.

Memory line in date order: Froebel, Dewey, Piaget, Sputnik, NAEYC, NGSS.

Theories underpinning early childhood science education

  • Constructivism (Piaget): children construct knowledge through interaction.
  • Social constructivism (Vygotsky): knowledge is socially constructed through interaction with others. The Zone of Proximal Development says that with scaffolding children can achieve more than they would alone, so science activity should be collaborative.

Process Skills

Science Process Skills in ECE

Six basic skills, five integrated skills and how to tell them apart.

Science process skills are the cognitive and practical skills children use to investigate and understand the world.

Basic science process skills

#SkillMeaningExample
1ObservationFoundation of all science: using the five senses to gather information. Describe colour, texture, smell, sound of objects in nature.Qualitative: 'the leaf is green'. Quantitative (with numbers): 'the leaf has 5 points'.
2ClassificationGrouping objects or events by shared propertiesSorting games, grouping animals by habitat, organising natural objects by size
3MeasurementQuantifying an observation. Start with non-standard units (handspans, blocks, cups), then standard units (cm, mL). Supports maths and precision.Measure a plant with handspans first
4CommunicationSharing findings through writing, drawing, graphs, tables, verbal reportsChildren draw observations, use picture charts, describe what they found
5PredictionAn evidence-based educated guess about future events, unlike random guessing'I think the ice will melt faster under the sun.'
6InferenceDrawing conclusions from evidence; goes beyond observation to interpretation. Children must distinguish observation from inference.Seeing wet soil and inferring that it has rained
Plain English
Observation = what you see. Inference = what you work out from it. Wet soil is the observation; 'it rained' is the inference.

Integrated science process skills

Higher-order skills that build on the basic ones, more appropriate for older early childhood learners (6 to 8 years).

  1. Controlling variables: identifying and managing factors that affect an experiment.
  2. Formulating hypotheses: proposing testable explanations for observations.
  3. Interpreting data: finding patterns and meaning in collected information.
  4. Defining operationally: defining concepts by what one observes or measures.
  5. Experimenting: planning and conducting a full investigation.

Likely exam question

Explain inquiry-based learning and describe its five stages.

Inquiry-based learning is the most developmentally appropriate method of ECE science education because it mirrors the natural way young children learn: through curiosity, questioning and experimentation.

  1. Wondering and questioning: an intriguing object sparks curiosity.
  2. Exploring and investigating: children freely explore materials and ideas, often creating their own investigations.
  3. Recording and representing: children draw, dictate or photograph observations.
  4. Discussing and explaining: children share findings, comparing results and ideas.
  5. Applying and extending: children apply new understandings to different contexts.

Likely exam question

Explain the discovery and demonstration methods. What makes a demonstration effective?

Discovery: open materials and minimal instructions so children discover concepts themselves. Builds intrinsic motivation, critical thinking and ownership of learning. The teacher guides, only asking probing questions or introducing vocabulary.

Demonstration: the teacher shows a concept and makes thinking visible through narration. It is most effective when it is:

  • followed immediately by children's own hands-on exploration;
  • preceded by children predicting the outcome;
  • repeated so all children see clearly;
  • connected to prior knowledge and everyday experiences.

Concept Maps

Concept Maps and Hands-on Activities

Concept map types, steps and benefits, plus principles and examples of hands-on activities.

Concept maps help visualise relationships between ideas. Hands-on activities give the concrete experiences young children need. Together they organise, teach and assess science understanding.

What is a concept map?

A visual diagram that shows relationships between concepts. First developed by Joseph Novak (1970s), based on an assessment tool. It has nodes (circles or boxes holding concepts) joined by labelled arrows that describe the relationship (called linking phrases or propositions).

Example
Living → Animals; Living → Plants; Plants → need → Water → causes → Growth.

Types of concept maps

TypeDescriptionBest for
Spider mapCentral concept in the centre, related ideas radiating outwardBrainstorming prior knowledge
Hierarchy mapConcepts from most general (top) to most specific (bottom)Taxonomy and classification
Flowchart mapConcepts in a sequenceProcesses such as the water cycle and life cycles
Cluster / bubble mapCentral ideas surrounded by linked related ideas, not a strict hierarchy

Developing a concept map for ECE science

  1. Identify the key concept.
  2. Brainstorm related ideas with children.
  3. Write each idea on a card or sticky note.
  4. Arrange the cards to show relationships.
  5. Draw labelled arrows between connected ideas and write linking words on the arrows.
  6. Review and revise the maps.
Plain English
Concept maps are never finished. They can always be revisited and revised.

Benefits of concept maps in ECE science

  • Assess prior knowledge of a new topic.
  • Make abstract relationships visible and tangible.
  • Encourage children to articulate their thinking.
  • Can be revisited and revised as learning progresses.
  • Support vocabulary development by connecting new words in meaningful contexts.

Children manipulate materials directly. Grounded in Piaget's belief that children learn most effectively through physical interaction with concrete materials.

Living and Non-Living Things

Basic Concepts in Science and Nature: Living and Non-Living

Organisms, MRS GREN, misconceptions, classification and ecosystems.

4.2 Living things and MRS GREN

A living thing (organism) is any entity that possesses and exhibits the characteristics of life. Teachers use the mnemonic MRS GREN (or MRS NERG).

LetterCharacteristicChild-friendly explanation
MMovementLiving things can move by themselves (animals walk; plants turn towards sunlight).
RRespirationLiving things release energy from food (breathing in animals; cellular respiration in plants).
SSensitivityLiving things respond to changes in their environment (plants towards light; animals feeling pain).
NNutritionLiving things need food to grow and have energy (animals eat; plants make food through photosynthesis).
EExcretionLiving things remove waste products from their bodies (animals urinate; plants release oxygen).
RReproductionLiving things can make more of their own kind (seeds become plants; animals have babies).
GGrowthLiving things grow and develop over time (seeds grow into trees; babies grow into adults).
Plain English
If it moves, breathes, senses, feeds, passes waste, has young and grows, it is alive. Memory line: Movement, Respiration, Sensitivity, Growth, Reproduction, Excretion, Nutrition.

4.3 Non-living things

Non-living things (abiotic factors) do not possess the characteristics of life. They have never been alive (rocks, water, air) or once were part of a living organism but no longer show life processes (wood, paper, dried leaves). They support living things and shape ecosystems.

4.4 Teaching the distinction in ECE

Common misconceptions. Young children classify by movement or obvious activity rather than biological criteria:

ObjectWhy children think it is alive
FireIt moves, grows and consumes oxygen
The SunIt seems to move across the sky and gives warmth
Robots / carsThey move and seem to 'do things'
Dead animals and plantsSome children do not recognise they were once living

Effective teaching strategies:

  • Sorting activities: sort rock, leaf, insect, toy car, seed, feather, plastic cup into 'living' and 'non-living' piles and discuss reasoning.
  • Nature walks: identify and label living and non-living things outdoors.
  • Life cycle explorations: grow caterpillars into butterflies or seeds into plants.
  • Concept maps: a class map of the characteristics of living and non-living things.
  • Songs and rhymes: reinforce the characteristics of life.

4.5 Classification of living things

GroupDescriptionExamples
AnimalsMove independently, consume other organisms for nutrition, breathe oxygendogs, birds, fish, insects
PlantsMake their own food by photosynthesis, generally rooted in one place, need sunlight, water and carbon dioxidetrees, grasses, flowers, ferns
FungiFeed by decomposing organic mattermushrooms, moulds
MicroorganismsTiny living things visible only under a microscopebacteria, amoeba, paramecium

4.6 Ecosystems and interdependence

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms interacting with each other and their non-living environment (a pond, a forest, a garden). Living and non-living things are interconnected and interdependent.

  • Food chain: who eats whom. Example: grass → grasshopper → frog → hawk.
  • Habitat: the natural home of an organism, giving food, shelter and water.
  • Adaptation: how living things suit their environment. Example: polar bears have thick fur for cold climates.

Define a living thing and state the seven characteristics of life (MRS GREN).

  • Adaptation: how living things are suited to their environment (polar bears have thick fur for cold climates).

Movement Education

Movement Education in ECE Centres

Meaning, scope, importance, curriculum and daily integration.

6.1 and 6.2 What movement education is

Movement education (also physical education or motor learning) is the process of learning to move and moving to learn. It is a child-centred approach to physical education that focuses on developing children's understanding and skill in moving their bodies. Unlike traditional PE (sport skills and competition), it focuses on four things:

FocusMeaningExamples
Body awarenessWhat the body can dostretch, curl, twist, balance, jump
Space awarenessWhere the body movespersonal space, general space, directions, pathways
EffortHow the body movesfast/slow, strong/light, sudden/sustained, bound/free flow
RelationshipsWith whom or what the body moveswith a partner, in a group, over/under an object

6.3 Importance in ECE

AreaContribution
PhysicalGross motor skills (running, jumping, climbing, throwing, catching); fine motor skills (manipulatives, craft, tools); cardiovascular health, muscle strength, bone density; healthy body weight, lower risk of childhood obesity
CognitiveMore blood flow to the brain, better readiness to learn; spatial awareness, laterality, directionality (precursors to reading and maths); working memory from following sequences; executive function from movement problem-solving
Social-emotionalCooperation, turn-taking, social skills; confidence, resilience, positive self-concept; less stress, anxiety and hyperactivity; dance supports emotional regulation and self-expression
Language and literacyPositional language (over, under, beside, between), directional language (forwards, backwards, sideways), descriptive language (quickly, slowly, smoothly, jerky); listening and speaking skills through instructions

6.5 Integrating movement in the daily routine

Movement should not be confined to one 'gym time'.

  • Morning movement rituals: greeting songs with actions, morning stretches.
  • Movement breaks between sedentary activities: 5-minute action songs or games.
  • Outdoor play: free play with natural and manufactured equipment.
  • Movement-based literacy and numeracy: jumping to count, moving to letters, acting out stories.
  • End-of-day calming movement: yoga poses, slow stretches, relaxation.

The Gymnasium

Characteristics of the Gymnasium

Size, flooring, walls, lighting, ventilation, safety, acoustics and organisation.

7.2 Definition and purpose

An ECE gymnasium (movement room, physical activity hall, indoor play space) is a purpose-built or adapted indoor space that provides a safe, stimulating environment for structured and unstructured physical activity. Its purposes:

  • Provide a weather-independent space for PE.
  • House and organise movement equipment safely.
  • Enable structured movement education programmes.
  • Support free, exploratory physical play.
  • Allow large group activities that need significant floor space.

7.3 Characteristics of an ideal ECE gymnasium

FeatureRequirement
SizeAt least 100 to 150 m² for a class of 20 to 25 children. Ceiling at least 3 to 4 m. Open floor plan, no fixed obstructions.
FlooringCushioned, non-slip (rubber, foam-backed vinyl, sprung wood). Shock-absorbing. Easy to clean. Marked lines for activities. No gaps, cracks or uneven surfaces. The floor is one of the most critical safety features.
WallsSmooth, rounded corners. Padded sections near fall or run areas. Display visual cues, prompts, children's art. Storage units or recessed shelving. Mirror panels on at least one wall.
LightingAmple natural light (large windows or skylights). Bright, even artificial light. Controls to adjust (bright for active play, dimmed for relaxation).
Ventilation and temperatureGood cross-ventilation. Air conditioning or fans in hot climates, heating in cold ones. Temperature ideally 18 to 22°C during activity.
SafetyPadded or rounded sharp corners and hard edges. Equipment stored safely (locked storage or high shelving). Clear emergency exits with signage. First aid kit. Fire extinguisher and smoke detectors. Regular safety inspections and maintenance.
AcousticsSound-absorbing ceiling tiles or panels to reduce echo. Acoustic wall panels. Clear acoustic zones for music without overwhelming other areas.
Plain English
Memory line, S F W L V S A: Size, Flooring, Walls, Lighting, Ventilation, Safety, Acoustics.

7.4 Organisation of the space

  • Defined zones: balance area, climbing area, games area, relaxation area.
  • Accessible storage: children can reach equipment independently under supervision.
  • Clear pathways between zones.
  • Visual cues: pictures, symbols and colour coding on walls and floors.

The teacher gives specific instructions about what to do, how and when. All children do the same activity at the same time.

  • Advantages: clear instructions ensure safety; effective for specific skills or safety procedures; manages large groups efficiently.
  • Disadvantages: limits individual creativity and problem-solving; not suited to diverse ability levels; can feel regimented.
  • When to use: teaching a new skill step by step, safety demonstrations, warm-up routines, introducing a new game's rules.
Example
'Everyone, stand on the blue dot. Watch me, I am going to show you how to do a forward roll. First, tuck your chin to your chest...'
📍 See Movement Teaching Methods (2)
Likely exam question

Discuss the guided discovery method with its advantages, disadvantages and an example.

The teacher poses movement problems or questions and children discover solutions through exploration. No single 'correct' response; all safe, appropriate solutions are accepted and celebrated.

  • Advantages: engaging and motivating; respects individual differences; develops creativity, problem-solving and intrinsic motivation.
  • Disadvantages: requires more time; hard to manage with large groups; needs high teacher responsiveness.
  • When to use: exploring movement concepts (space, effort, body awareness), creative movement and dance, movement problem-solving.
Example
'Can you find THREE different ways to cross the room without using your feet?'

Likely exam question

What is reciprocal teaching? State its advantages and disadvantages and the age from which it suits children.

Children work in pairs or small groups. One practises a skill while a partner observes and gives feedback using criteria the teacher provides (often through a visual card). Suited from about 5 years.

  • Advantages: develops communication and observation; immediate feedback; cooperation; frees the teacher to help individuals.
  • Disadvantages: needs sufficient language and social skills; feedback may be inaccurate; careful pairing needed.
📍 See Movement Teaching Methods (4)
Likely exam question

Explain the cooperative learning approach and the stations/circuit approach.

Cooperative learning: small groups work to achieve shared movement goals and all members are responsible for each other's learning. The teacher assigns roles (coach, observer, encourager, equipment manager) and gives structured tasks. Benefits: social skills, communication, leadership, inclusion.

Stations/circuit: the space is set up with several stations, each on a different skill. Children rotate through all stations for a set time. Advantages: everyone is active at once, differentiated stations, variety. Management tips: clear visual instructions, rotation signals (bell, music, clap), demonstrate each station first.

Likely exam question

Describe story-based movement and say for which ages it is most effective.

The teacher narrates a story and children interpret it through movement. Example: 'We are going on a bear hunt: walk through the tall grass (swish arms), splash through the river (stomp feet), climb up the mountain (reach and pull).'

Advantages: highly engaging, integrates language and literacy, uses imagination, accessible to all abilities. Most effective for ages 3 to 5, introducing concepts in a fun, low-pressure context.

Likely exam question

Explain how music and dance are used to teach movement.

  • Teacher-led dance: teacher demonstrates, children follow.
  • Structured dance sequences: a simple sequence of steps to a song.
  • Free dance: children move freely, expressing their own interpretation.
  • Props: ribbons, scarves, hoops and balloons enhance creative movement.

Music gives rhythm, motivation and structure.

Likely exam question

State seven principles for effective movement education teaching.

  1. Every child participates (minimise waiting).
  2. Differentiation (including children with disabilities).
  3. Positive reinforcement (praise effort, creativity, improvement).
  4. Safety first (equipment checks, clear rules).
  5. Use of voice (clear, simple, vary volume and tone).
  6. Positioning (see all children, move around).
  7. Transitions (plan efficient changes between activities).

The Basic Principles in Game Education and Teaching

Classification, value, seven principles, teaching sequence and conflict.

11.2 Definition and classification

A game is a structured form of play with rules, goals and interactions between participants.

Classified byTypes
Organisationindividual, partner, small-group, large-group
Activity typeactive, quiet, creative, cognitive
Structurestructured (fixed rules), semi-structured (flexible rules), unstructured play
Objectivecompetitive (a winner), cooperative (all work together), non-competitive (no winners or losers)

11.3 Value of games in ECE

AreaValue
PhysicalFundamental movement skills (running, jumping, throwing, catching, dodging); cardiovascular health, muscles, coordination; contribute to the recommended 60+ minutes of physical activity per day
CognitiveRules, logic and consequences; planning, decision-making, flexible thinking; attention, concentration, working memory; early mathematical thinking (counting, sorting, strategy)
SocialCooperative play, turn-taking, sharing, respectful competition; following agreed rules; conflict resolution; sense of community and belonging
EmotionalResilience (lose gracefully, win with humility); safe experience of excitement, suspense, disappointment; confidence and self-efficacy

11.4 Seven basic principles of game education

PrincipleMeaning
1. Developmentally appropriate practiceMatch games to children's physical, cognitive, social and emotional abilities. Too complex frustrates; too simple bores.
2. InclusionEvery child participates meaningfully: adapt rules for physical disabilities; alternative roles; avoid early elimination; be sensitive to culture and use games from diverse traditions.
3. SafetyPhysical and emotional: hazard-free surfaces, age-appropriate equipment in good repair, clear rules, positive climate (no bullying, ridicule or exclusion).
4. Joyfulness and intrinsic motivationGames should be fun. Playing for the joy of it lasts longer than playing to win or earn a reward.
5. ProgressionSimple to complex: start with no-fail cooperative versions, add rules and challenges gradually, let children modify rules, move from individual to partner to small group to large group.
6. Maximum participationAll children active as much as possible: multiple balls or hoops, tag games where tagged players rejoin quickly, small-group games.
7. Learning through reflectionBrief group discussion after games: What was challenging? How did we solve it as a team? What rule could be changed? How did it feel when your team scored?
Plain English
Memory line, D I S J P M R: Developmentally appropriate, Inclusion, Safety, Joyfulness, Progression, Maximum participation, Reflection.

11.6 Managing conflict in games

Conflict is natural (disputed calls, rule arguments, occasional cheating) and a valuable learning opportunity.

  • Model calm, fair resolution: 'It looks like you both saw it differently. Let's talk about what a fair solution might be.'
  • Use pre-agreed rules to settle disputes.
  • Encourage children to find their own solutions before you step in.
  • Celebrate good sportsmanship publicly.

Likely exam question

How does the principle of inclusion apply to games?

  • Adapt rules for children with physical disabilities.
  • Provide alternative roles for children who cannot take part physically on a given day.
  • Avoid games that eliminate children early and leave them sitting out for long.
  • Be sensitive to cultural backgrounds and choose games from diverse cultural traditions.

Likely exam question

Outline the sequence for organising and teaching a game.

  1. Preparation: set up equipment before children arrive; know the game.
  2. Introduction: gather children, explain the purpose briefly.
  3. Demonstration: model the game (use children as helpers).
  4. Explanation of rules: keep simple, safety rules first.
  5. Practice run: a slow, abbreviated version.
  6. Full game: observe participation and enjoyment.
  7. Modification: adjust rules, teams or equipment.
  8. Reflection: brief discussion at the end.
